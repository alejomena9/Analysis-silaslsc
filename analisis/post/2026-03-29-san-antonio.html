A Clear Intensity Gap

The biggest story of this match was the gap in intensity. San Antonio were sharp and committed — pressing hard, fighting for every loose ball, shooting from range, and winning the vast majority of crossed and contested balls. Lexington, by contrast, looked passive and slow, struggling to break through San Antonio's lines for long stretches.

What the numbers say

Lexington had more of the ball, more touches in the box and a higher xG. The chances were created where the plan said they would be; the difference was in the duels, the second balls and the finishing.

The Pocket We Identified Actually Opened Up

Even in a slow performance, Lexington found the exact space flagged before kickoff — the pocket behind San Antonio's marking midfielders. Alfredo Midence received well there, created room for himself and set up Phillip Goodrum for a shot that had a real chance of being the opener.

The overconfidence we'd noted in San Antonio's centre-backs showed up too: Goodrum had the clearest chance of the match off a second header when Marcus Epps won the first ball back into the box, but the finish drifted just wide.

Where It Went Wrong

Lexington's passivity got worse as the game went on — the second half was noticeably slower and less intense than the first. San Antonio only grew more consistent and aggressive, and that difference produced their second goal: a change of rhythm through midfield exposed Molloy and Ferri centrally, precisely because San Antonio's two full-backs were pushed so high and wide. With Lexington poorly set in that moment, Dmitrii Erofeev was left with a clean, unchallenged strike.

Conclusion

The tactical picture from the pre-match report held up — the space existed exactly where it was expected to be. What decided the result was intensity, not the game plan: San Antonio simply played the match at a higher gear.
