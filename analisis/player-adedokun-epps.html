Michael Adedokun & Marcus Epps

Wide Forwards
In One Line

Their level didn't disappear — the system around them did, turning a connected partnership into two isolated players.

Where Did Last Season's Level Go?

Where did the excellent level that Michael Adedokun and Marcus Epps showed last season actually go? It didn't evaporate. While neither is having a great season individually, that's not entirely on them — they're playing in a very different system from a year ago, one that simply doesn't help them the way the old one did.

Two Different Profiles, One Shared Strength

On the surface Michael and Marcus look like similar profiles, but they're genuinely different players. Michael is a blistering runner who can beat any defender on pure speed, while Marcus is a natural combination player who can create danger in less than a meter of space. The real reason for last season's success, though, wasn't either of those individual traits — it was volume. Lexington attacked with heavy numbers on both flanks; Sargent and Joe would push forward at the same time constantly, and it was never a problem because the team had an excellent structure to recover if the ball was lost. That mutual release between the full-backs stretched the opposing team wide enough that Lexington could attack through up to six different lanes at once.

A More Static System, More Isolated Players

This year Lexington changed systems — the team now plays in a more static, less volatile shape. On paper that looks like it should reduce risk, but in practice it creates islands: disconnected pockets of players that end up being more dangerous for Lexington itself, not less. What used to be controlled chaos now looks closer to a robotic sequence, where every player has one assigned movement and nothing beyond it.

The Movement That Used To Exist

Consider a specific sequence from last season: Michael receives the ball on the touchline while Marcus moves inside-out. What does that create? An interior pocket of space that Michael can then use. It also worked the other way — Michael moving inside-out for Marcus — and that inside-out movement wasn't just for the two of them; it decongested the midfield and opened room for players like Firmino, Ferri or Sargent, one of whom actually finished a goal from exactly this pattern.

This season that connection looks very different. In a near-identical situation, the same supporting movement simply isn't there anymore — it doesn't exist in the way it used to — and that leaves both wingers exposed to losing the ball or forced into crossing toward a Philip who's often standing alone in the box.

Isolation Hides Their Real Qualities

This lack of volume also limits what Michael and Marcus can actually show. Being so isolated from one another leaves both of them constantly outnumbered out wide, and that exposure is the real problem — when either of them gets numerical parity on the flank, no defender can stop them one-on-one, as we saw in a specific sequence with Marcus, or in another where Michael, with a bit of interior support, beat his marker for pace on a play that could easily have ended in a goal.

The Bigger Picture

This system doesn't just affect Michael and Marcus — it's also holding back the player who, by the underlying data, was Lexington's most consistent performer last season.
