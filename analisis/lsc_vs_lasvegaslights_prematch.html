Offensive Structure: 4-4-2 Diamond

Las Vegas Lights play a 4-4-2 diamond, not a flat line. Ybarra (6) sits deeper as the holding base, two midfielders occupy the interior channels, and Christian Pinzón (11) plays as the diamond's advanced tip — the player constantly looking for the pass between the lines, connecting build-up to attack.

Build-Up: 2-3-1-4

In build-up, Las Vegas shift into a 2-3-1-4: the two center-backs stay short and fixed as the base, the interior line of three works the central channel looking for the pass between the lines, and both full-backs push hard to provide the width the diamond doesn't naturally offer. Both forwards pin the opposing center-backs to keep the middle open.

Key Weakness: Space In Behind

This is the clearest crack. Because both full-backs push high to provide width, and the diamond's interior midfielders have long recovery runs, the space behind the full-backs opens up the moment they lose the ball. Neither center-back is especially comfortable defending in open space — strong in the duel, but they struggle against pace.

Alert for them · Opportunity for LSC

A fast, mobile forward attacking that space the instant they lose the ball is the most effective way to punish this structure.

Attack Loaded Down The Right

Once Pinzón receives between the lines, Las Vegas immediately look to switch the point of attack to the right — their preferred zone, generating 43% of the team's offensive actions. It's a combination between the full-back and the right-sided interior midfielder, looking for depth before the opposing block can reset.

They need a moment of individual quality: a fast start, a late intervention, or a set-piece error from the opponent.

Prediction

Press the build-up, attack the space behind the full-back, and never lose focus in the first and final minutes. Las Vegas control the ball centrally through a well-organized diamond — their crack is in defensive transition, not build-up, and that's where our attacking plan should live.
