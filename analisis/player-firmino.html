Nick Firmino

#10 → False Right Winger
In One Line

Moved off a heavily-marked #10 spot to a false right-winger role, Firmino stopped being the one forced to create — and started being the one defenses can't track.

A Difficult Start As A #10

Nick Firmino began the season as a #10 in a 4-2-3-1, playing just behind the striker. In that role he wasn't finding success — he was being tightly marked by rival midfielders and defenders, struggled to receive behind the opposing double pivot, and simply wasn't influential in the way the team needed. He lost a lot of balls, was constantly in positional inferiority, and misplaced far too many passes.

The Move To False Right Winger

Later in the season the coaching staff moved him to a false right-winger role. When the team has the ball, he tucks inside as an interior, and together with the two 6s and Braudilio — who drops into that #10 space — he forms a diamond shape centrally. That positioning lets him receive with far more freedom, since he's no longer camped in a marked zone but arriving into space as it opens, which generates room not just for himself but for Braudilio as well.

Receiving In Superiority, Not Inferiority

The role change also lets him receive far more often in positional superiority, which in turn gives him the platform for shots from range and considerably more presence inside the box. He's no longer the one who has to single-handedly generate the team's play — that responsibility is now shared across the diamond that forms around him.

A Flank Defenders Can't Solve

This shift also opens real space out wide, because the opposing full-back no longer knows whether to track Firmino as he tucks inside or hold his width and wait for the overlapping full-back to arrive.

Why It Matters

A single positional tweak turned a heavily-marked, low-impact #10 into a free, high-superiority interior — and the ripple effects show up all over the pitch.
