In One Line

Phoenix are elite in possession, but they've lost exactly two ways this season — and Lexington has the personnel to attack them both.

One Of The League's Best In Possession

Phoenix Rising arrive in Lexington as one of the best possession teams in the league, and it isn't an accident — they exit cleanly from the back, avoid careless mistakes, and circulate the ball through all three thirds of the pitch with a comfort that unsettles most opponents. Tactically they set up in a 4-2-3-1, but in the possession phase that shape shifts into a 3-2-2-3, looking for numerical superiority in the build-up and occupying passing lanes intelligently.

Two Proven Ways They've Been Beaten

Looking at how Phoenix has actually lost this season, two clear paths stand out. The first is to actively take the ball away from them rather than simply contesting it — that's exactly what Colorado Springs did in Phoenix's most recent defeat. The difference wasn't playing better with the ball; it was physically taking it off them. Phoenix are deeply uncomfortable without possession, and that discomfort is exploitable with sustained, aggressive pressure.

The second path is the one Louisville used: beating them in fast transition, scoring in as little as three or four passes. This route is riskier, since there's no way to know exactly when that transition opportunity will appear during the match, but it's a proven method all the same.

A Point Of Self-Criticism

This is also where Lexington needs to be honest with itself. Against Monterrey, both of the opponent's goals came from exactly this kind of poorly closed transition from attack to defense. Phoenix move in synchronized blocks and flood the box very quickly the moment they win the ball back — if Lexington repeats that same vulnerability, the match can get complicated in waves, regardless of which path is chosen to attack them.

The Tools Already Exist

The good news is that Lexington already has the right personnel for both paths. Ferri and Molloy's passing range is ideal for balls in behind, exploiting the disorder Phoenix shows right after losing possession. Greene, Epps and Zengue bring the flank speed needed to run into that space before Phoenix can reorganize. And Braudilio and Goodrum are two players who consistently find pockets inside the opposing box exactly at the moment of transition.

Game Plan By Phase

In possession: hold the ball, look for long attacking sequences, and isolate Epps for a 1v1. In the defense-to-attack transition: speed is everything — respond fast and finish clinically. In the defensive phase: sit in a compact mid-block that forces Phoenix to build down their weaker left side. And in the attack-to-defense transition — the moment that hurt Lexington most against Monterrey — the instruction is clear: delay Phoenix's counter as much as possible, using home-field tactical fouls when necessary. Two paths, one safer and one riskier but potentially decisive — the plan is to keep both live depending on how the match unfolds.
